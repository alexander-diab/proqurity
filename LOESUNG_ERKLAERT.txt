================================================================================
WHAT WE BUILT, AND WHAT ACTUALLY SOLVED IT
Written for people who do not work with software.
No jargon. Where a technical word is unavoidable, it is explained once.
================================================================================


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. THE SITUATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A large company buys things. Chemicals, packaging, spare parts.

Every step leaves a record: the order was created, the supplier confirmed,
the goods arrived, the invoice was paid. Hundreds of thousands of these.

Sometimes, after the order has been placed, the price changes.

That may be completely fine. Suppliers are allowed to raise prices, under
conditions written into the contract - usually something like "you must
tell us thirty days in advance."

Or it may not be fine at all.

The company's own systems cannot tell the difference. They record that the
price changed. They do not know what the contract says, and they have never
seen the email where the supplier announced it.

So today a person does it by hand. Open the order. Find the contract in a
folder. Search the mailbox. Compare the dates. Decide. About twenty minutes,
per case, and there are hundreds of cases.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. WHY THIS IS GENUINELY HARD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Three kinds of question, and only one of them was ever difficult.

  "How much did we spend with this supplier?"
      Easy. Any reporting tool answers this.

  "What does the price clause in this contract say?"
      Easy. Any document search answers this.

  "On which orders was the price raised without the required notice?"
      Nothing answers this.

The third question is not harder in the way a maths problem is harder.
It is harder because the answer does not live in one place.

It needs a date from the purchasing system, a deadline from a PDF contract,
and an announcement date from an email - all for the SAME order, connected.

None of those three systems knows the other two exist.

That gap is the whole problem. Everything we built is a way to close it.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. THE IDEA THAT SOLVED IT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We stopped treating the records, the contracts and the emails as three
separate piles, and connected them into one map.

Think of a map of a city rather than three separate address books.

On this map, every order line is a place. Roads lead out from it:

    - one road to the supplier
    - from the supplier, a road to the contract we signed with them
    - from the contract, a road to the specific clause about prices
    - roads to every email and document about that order
    - roads to every event: created, confirmed, delivered, paid
    - and from each event, a road to the person who did it

Now the impossible question becomes a walk.

Start at the order. Walk to the contract, read "thirty days." Walk to the
email, read "announced on the eleventh, effective on the fourteenth."
Subtract. Three days. Three is less than thirty.

That is the finding. And critically, the route is exact. You either can walk
from this order to its contract, or that road does not exist - in which case
the honest answer is "there is no rule here to check against," which is
itself worth knowing.

The technical name for this kind of map is a "graph database." That is the
only piece of jargon that matters, and that is all it means: things, and the
connections between them, stored so the connections can be followed.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. THE PART MOST PEOPLE GET WRONG
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The obvious modern approach is: throw all the documents at an AI and ask it.

That approach searches by resemblance. It finds the documents that LOOK most
like your question.

We measured what happens when you do that here, and it is worth understanding,
because it is the reason the map exists.

We asked: "how many days' notice was given on order 4508048711?"

Searching all the documents by resemblance returned a price announcement
letter from a COMPLETELY DIFFERENT SUPPLIER - and rated it a BETTER match
than the correct letter.

Why? Because all price announcement letters look alike. Same layout, same
polite phrases, same structure. They differ only in the order number and the
dates - which is precisely the detail that resemblance-based search handles
worst.

So the machine would have been confidently, fluently wrong. It would have
quoted a real document, from a real supplier, about a different order.

In an audit, that is not a small error. That is an accusation against the
wrong company.

Following the map instead of guessing by resemblance is what makes it correct.

  Resemblance search is good at finding SIMILAR things.
  It is bad at finding RELATED things.
  An audit needs related.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. WHERE THE AI IS ALLOWED TO WORK - AND WHERE IT IS NOT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is the design decision that makes the result trustworthy, so it is worth
being precise.

  THE COMPUTER DOES THE FACTS

    Which documents belong to this order      - by following the map
    The announcement date, the effective date - read from the email
    How many days between them                - subtraction
    What the contract requires                - stored as a number
    Whether the person was within their limit - comparison
    The verdict itself                        - a rule, written down

  THE AI DOES ONE THING

    It writes the short paragraph at the bottom of the report that explains
    the finding to a human being in ordinary language.

    It is explicitly forbidden from introducing any number, date, name or
    amount that was not already computed above it.

Why draw the line there?

Because a report that looks official and contains a number nobody can trace
back to a source document is worse than useless. It is dangerous. Someone
will act on it.

Every figure on our report can be clicked back to the email or contract it
came from. The one paragraph that is written rather than calculated is
labelled as such, at the bottom of the page, where a reader has already seen
the evidence.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. WHAT COMES OUT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Someone types an order number - or asks a question in plain English - and
gets a one-page report.

Every case lands in one of four boxes:

  DOCUMENTED           The price rise happened, and it followed the rules.
                       Notice was given in time. Nothing to do.
                       79 cases.

  SUSPECTED VIOLATION  The document exists, we read it, and it contradicts
                       the contract. Notice was three days where thirty
                       were required.
                       34 cases.

  UNEXPLAINED          The price rise happened and there is no document
                       at all. Not an accusation - a question that needs
                       asking.
                       44 cases.

  NOT ASSESSABLE       There is no framework contract with this supplier,
                       so there is no rule to measure against. We refuse
                       to judge it.
                       162 cases.

That last box matters more than it looks. A tool that always produces an
answer cannot be trusted, because it has no way of telling you when it does
not know. Roughly nine out of ten purchase orders have nothing interesting on
them at all, and the system says so and stops.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. HOW WE KNOW IT IS RIGHT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Before building any of it, the correct answer for all 319 cases was written
down separately.

The system was then run and compared against that list.

  319 out of 319 correct. No case put in the wrong box.

Read that result carefully, because it is easy to oversell.

It proves the machinery works from end to end - the map is connected properly,
the dates are read correctly, the arithmetic is right, the rule is applied
consistently.

It does NOT prove this would work on a real company's messy documents. Our
contracts and emails were generated for the test, because no company publishes
its real ones. Real documents are inconsistent, badly scanned and full of
exceptions.

That is the honest state of it: the method is proven, the data is not yet real.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. WHAT WE ARE STILL NOT SURE ABOUT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Three things we report but cannot prove:

  - The percentage tolerance check. In our data every price rise was above
    the allowed tolerance, so that test never actually decided anything.

  - The approval-limit check. We found 59 price changes entered by someone
    whose authority was below the order value - including three entered by
    warehouse staff with no approval authority whatsoever. It is a striking
    finding. It also has no verified correct answer, so we report it as an
    observation, never as a proven breach.

  - Real documents. See section 7.

All three are marked as unproven on the report itself, in the footer, on
every single page.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
9. THE ONE-SENTENCE VERSION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We connected the purchasing records, the contracts and the emails into a
single map, so that a question no single system could answer - "was this
price rise allowed?" - becomes a route that can be walked, checked, and
shown to a human being on one page.
